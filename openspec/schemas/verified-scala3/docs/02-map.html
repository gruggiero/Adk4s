    Chapter 2 · Orientation

    The map

    The whole workflow, once, at low resolution — so that when later chapters zoom in you always know which organ you are looking at and what it is connected to. Nothing here is deep. Everything here is load-bearing.

    Three layers, one loop

    verified-scala3 has exactly three moving parts, and confusion almost always comes from mixing them up:

    LayerWhat it isWhen it runsChapter
    Artifacts Eight documents produced before any code is written, each a prerequisite of the next. Once per change. 3, 4, 5
    The apply loop Fourteen steps (0–13) that take one spec from concept check to committed, verified code. Once per spec, repeated. 6, 8
    The rings Ten independent verification mechanisms, 0–9, invoked from inside the loop. Per spec, conditionally. 7
    Why it is designed this way

    The nesting is deliberate: a change contains specs; a spec traverses rings. This is what makes the method survive real projects — the unit of human approval is one spec, not one pull request with fourteen files, and the unit of verification is one mechanism, not “did CI go green”. When something fails you know exactly which spec and which ring, and the blast radius is one commit.

    Vocabulary you need immediately

    TermMeaning in this workflow
    change A unit of work with a directory under openspec/changes/<name>/ holding its artifacts. Roughly “a feature”, but scoped by capability rather than by ticket.
    spec A delta specification for one capability: specs/<capability>/spec.md. A change has one or more. Delta means it states what is ADDED/MODIFIED/REMOVED, not the whole world.
    requirement One normative claim inside a spec, with SHALL/MUST and Given/When/Then, plus scenarios.
    concept Overloaded on purpose, and chapter 4 untangles it: a behavioural concept (a named unit of user-facing behaviour) or a type concept (an entry in the type inventory).
    proof obligation A row binding one claim to the mechanism that keeps it true. Chapter 5.
    ring One verification mechanism, numbered 0–9, cheapest and most fundamental first.
    gate A mandatory stop where a human approves before work continues. Three per spec.
    baseline The commit SHA a spec starts from. Every diff the workflow computes is against it.
    discharged The third clause of correctness (chapter 12), after bound and resolved: an obligation's artifact was observed to run green, in this session, with a recorded command — not merely that it exists.

    Layer 1 · The artifacts

    Eight documents, each requiring the ones before it. Click through them — the order is the argument.

    Rule · nothing skips ahead

    Each artifact declares its prerequisites and the workflow enforces them. You cannot write specs without a capability profile (you would be guessing at the stack), cannot produce an implementation order while any spec is FAIL, and cannot start coding without a complete proof-obligations table.

    Layer 2 · The apply loop

    Then, one spec at a time, fourteen steps. Three of them stop dead and wait for a human. Click any step:

    Failure mode · “I'll just do the next one, it's simple”

    The schema names this in capital letters because it is the most common way the discipline dissolves. One spec, one checkpoint, one approval. The reason is not ceremony: it is that a checkpoint is where a human supplies the adequacy judgement no mechanism can (chapter 1's third row), and batching four specs into one review reliably means three of them are approved unread.

    Layer 3 · The rings

    Rings are numbered by fundamentality, not by execution order — a distinction that trips up everyone once. Ring 0 is the compiler; ring 9 is production telemetry.

    RingMechanismCatchesApplies
    0Compilationtype errors, inexhaustive matches (escalated to errors)always
    1Lint + dangerous-pattern scanvar, null, throws, unsafe .get, catch-alls, castsalways
    2Architecture ruleslayer violations, unsealed domain types, raw primitives at boundarieswhen configured
    3Property & scenario testsbehavioural defects over a domain of inputsmandatory
    4Wire/persistence compatibilitybreaking a format that old data or peers depend onif serialization touched
    5Mutation testingtests that execute code without asserting anything meaningfulif tooling present
    6Formal verificationviolated pre/postconditions on pure functions, for all inputspure modules
    7Model checkingdistributed invariants: ordering, idempotency, replay equivalenceoptional
    8Adversarial reviewcode that passes the tests while violating the specmandatory
    9Telemetry / runtime monitorstemporal properties violated in productionif stack present

    Execution order inside the loop is 0 → 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 8 → 5 → 6/7 → 9. Ring 8 jumps the queue for a reason you can now predict: it is cheap and it changes code, so running it before mutation means mutation measures the final suite instead of a suite that is about to change. Chapter 7.

    Analogy · the Swiss cheese model

    James Reason's model of accident causation: each defence layer has holes, and an accident requires the holes to line up. Adding a thicker slice of the same cheese does not help — the holes are in the same places. Adding a different cheese does. Ten rings are ten differently-holed slices: the compiler cannot see a wrong constant, mutation cannot see a missing requirement, the adversarial reviewer cannot see a race condition, the model checker cannot see an ugly API. Each covers what its neighbours structurally cannot.

    Why it is designed this way · the shape of the cost curve

    Read the apply loop left to right and notice the cost gradient. Steps 0–2 are cheap thinking. Steps 3–6 are ordinary development. Steps 8–11 are expensive machine time and scarce human attention. A defect caught at step 0 costs a sentence; the same defect caught at step 9 costs a full re-run of every ring after the fix. This is the classic defect-cost-escalation curve, and the entire ordering of this workflow is an attempt to shift detection leftward — which is also why the two inner gates sit before implementation rather than after.

    Where the humans are

    Exactly three stops per spec, and they are not evenly weighted:

    1. After the typed contract — “are these the right types, signatures and errors?” Cheapest possible moment to be wrong.
    2. After the test oracle — “do these tests actually encode the spec, and has each one been seen to fail?”
    3. At the checkpoint — “is this spec genuinely done, and do I accept the residual risks it lists?”

    On a simple, low-risk spec the first two may be tiered into a single presentation — both steps still execute in full, but the human stops once. That is a deliberate concession to attention economics: a gate a human has stopped reading is worse than no gate, because it produces a record of approval that never happened. Chapter 8.

    What you are not being asked to do

    A common first reaction is “this is enormous”. Note what is conditional:

    • Rings 4, 5, 6, 7, 9 apply only when relevant and available — a typical pure-logic change runs 0, 1, 2, 3, 8.
    • The behavioural concept registry (chapter 4) is optional; every rule that depends on it is conditional on the directory existing.
    • The typed contract can be minimal for a pure refactor.
    • Missing tooling never silently degrades the claim — it is recorded as a skipped ring with a stated correctness impact, or converted into a manually-reviewed obligation.

    What is never conditional: Ring 0, Ring 1, Ring 3, Ring 8, the typed contract for code-changing specs, the proof-obligations table, and the checkpoint.

    Check yourself

    Before moving on, you should be able to say

    • The three layers and which repeats inside which.
    • Roughly what each of the eight artifacts is for, and why the order is forced.
    • The three human gates and what question each answers.
    • Why ring numbering and ring execution order differ.
